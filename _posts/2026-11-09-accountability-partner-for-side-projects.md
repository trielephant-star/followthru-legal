At work, someone notices when a project stalls. On a side project, nobody does. That’s why accountability is one of the most effective changes you can make: it brings back the gentle outside pressure that day jobs come with for free.

But “get an accountability partner” is easier said than done. Friends get busy, groups fizzle out after three weeks, and posting your goals publicly can feel like signing up for embarrassment. Here are the main options, what each is good at, and how to make whichever you choose actually last.

- Accountability works because someone expects to hear from you, not because they push you
- The options: a friend, a small group, building in public, a coach, or an app
- Most partnerships fail on structure, not effort: agree on when and what you’ll share
- Keep it kind. Shame makes people go quiet, not get back to work
{: .summary}

## Why accountability works (and what it isn’t)

An accountability partner isn’t a boss, a cheerleader or a critic. Their job is much simpler: they expect to hear from you.

That expectation does a surprising amount of work. Knowing that someone will ask “so, did you write the product descriptions?” on Sunday makes it much easier to actually sit down on Thursday. It turns a private intention into a small social commitment.

What it isn’t: pressure, nagging or judgment. The best accountability feels like a friendly question, not a performance review. If missing a week makes you dread the check-in, the setup needs to change.

## Your options, compared

| Option | Good for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A friend who is also building | Warm, personal, free | Both of you get busy at the same time |
| A small builders’ group | Variety, ideas, a bit of peer pressure | Groups often fade after a few weeks |
| Building in public | Reach, feedback, meeting people | Public misses can feel embarrassing |
| A paid coach | Expert advice and real commitment | Expensive for a side project |
| An accountability app | Daily, always there, no awkwardness | Easy to ignore if it only sends reminders |

### 1. A friend who is also building something

The classic option, and often the best. Look for someone who is working on their own project, so the support goes both ways. It doesn’t matter if their project is completely different from yours. A novelist and an app maker can keep each other honest just fine.

Keep it light: a ten-minute call or a short message thread once a week.

### 2. A small group of builders

Three to five people is the sweet spot: enough that one person being busy doesn’t end it, small enough that everyone gets attention. You can find people in communities like Indie Hackers, the r/SideProject subreddit, local meetups or the Discord or Slack group for your niche.

When you post, be specific: “Looking for 2 to 3 people building something after work. Weekly check-in, Sundays, 20 minutes, async in a group chat.” Specific requests get much better replies than “anyone want to be accountability buddies?”

### 3. Building in public

Sharing your progress on social media, a blog or a newsletter adds a large, loose kind of accountability, and can bring early users and useful feedback.

The risk is that public accountability can tip into public pressure. Some tools and communities lean into “ship or shame”. That works for a few people and backfires for many: after a missed week, it feels easier to go quiet than to admit it. If you build in public, share what you did rather than promising what you’ll do, and let yourself have quiet weeks.

### 4. A coach

A business or productivity coach brings real expertise and a strong commitment (you’re paying, after all). For a serious venture that might be worth it. For most side projects, especially early on, it’s more than you need.

### 5. An accountability app

Apps solve the biggest weakness of human partners: availability. An app is there at 6 am and at 11 pm, never has a busy week and is never awkward to message after you’ve gone quiet.

The catch is that many apps only send reminders, and reminders are easy to swipe away. Look for an app that:

- asks how it went, instead of only telling you to do something,
- helps you break the next step down when you’re stuck,
- remembers your project, so you don’t start from zero each time,
- and forgives missed days instead of resetting a streak to zero.

That’s what we designed [FollowThru](/) to do. You pick one small Move a day, Momo checks in each evening and remembers how it went, and every Sunday you get a short Builder Report. It works well on its own or alongside a human partner.

## How to make accountability actually stick

Most accountability partnerships don’t fail because people stop caring. They fail because nobody agreed on the structure, so check-ins drift, then stop. Agree on these four things at the start:

1. **When.** A fixed day and time, like Sunday evening. “Whenever” fades fast.
2. **What you’ll share.** Keep it to three lines: what I planned, what I did, what’s next.
3. **How long.** Try it for four weeks, then decide together whether to keep going.
4. **What happens after a miss.** Agree upfront that a missed week is no big deal and you just pick up again. This one matters most.

A simple weekly check-in message can be this short:

> Planned: 3 product listings. Did: 2, photos for the third still to do. Next week: finish the listing and set up shipping.

Make your next step so small that you’d be slightly embarrassed not to do it. Then the check-in becomes a chance to share a win, not explain a miss.

## If you can’t find anyone

That’s common, and it doesn’t mean you can’t have accountability. Some options that need nobody else:

- **A weekly review with yourself.** Every Sunday, write down what you did, what got in the way and next week’s three steps. It sounds too simple to work, but writing it down creates a record you’ll want to keep going.
- **A visible log.** A notebook or a note on your phone where every finished step goes. Seeing the list grow is its own kind of accountability.
- **An app that checks in on you.** Something that asks each evening and remembers the answer, so there’s always someone (or a very patient sloth) expecting to hear from you.

The form matters less than the fact that something, or someone, notices when you show up. Start with whichever option feels easiest this week. You can always add another later.
