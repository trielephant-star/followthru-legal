To finish what you start, pick one small step each day that takes 15 to 60 minutes, ends with something you can point to, and moves you toward a finish line you wrote down. Do the step, note what you did, then choose tomorrow's. A missed day just means you pick up again.

If you have a job, a family or a full calendar, finishing a project is rarely about willpower. It is about how big the next thing looks. "Build the website" is a mountain. "Write the three lines at the top of the home page" is a Tuesday-night task.

This post explains the method step by step. You can start tonight with a pen and paper. If you want to know why projects stall in the first place, [why you don't finish side projects](/blog/why-you-dont-finish-side-projects/) covers the four usual reasons.

- Write a one-sentence finish line before you do anything else
- Break the road to it into steps that fit in one sitting
- Do one step a day and write down what you did
- Look back once a week and choose next week's steps
- A missed day changes nothing: start again with a smaller step
{: .summary}

## What is the one-small-step-a-day method?

It is a daily routine with four parts: a finish line, a list of small steps, one step done per day, and a short weekly look back. That is all.

It is built for people whose time comes in small, unpredictable pieces. You do not need a free weekend or a burst of motivation. You need to know what to do when you sit down, which is where most evenings are lost.

## How do I write a finish line?

A finish line is one sentence that says what "done" looks like for the version you will ship first. It should be something you could show another person.

Some examples:

- Mug shop: "Five mug listings live in my shop, with photos and shipping set up."
- Cooking channel: "Three videos published, each under eight minutes."
- Workout app: "A friend can log a workout on their own phone and see it the next day."
- Newsletter: "Issue one sent to ten people I know."

If your sentence has the word "and" more than twice, it is probably two finish lines. Pick one and put the other on a "later" list.

## How do I break the project into small steps?

Work backwards from the finish line. Ask: "What would have to be true right before this is done?" Write that down, then ask the same question again. Keep going until each item is something you could finish in one sitting.

A good step has three features:

1. **It starts with a verb.** "Photograph the blue mug," not "photos."
2. **It fits in 15 to 60 minutes.** Pick the length by your energy that day.
3. **It ends with a thing.** A drafted page, a recorded clip, a working button.

| Too big | Small enough |
| --- | --- |
| Set up my shop | Write the title and description for mug one |
| Plan my videos | List five video ideas and circle one |
| Work on the app | Make the "save workout" button store a workout |
| Do marketing | Write one post announcing the shop opening |

You do not need to plan every step on day one. Ten steps ahead is plenty. You will see the next ten once you have done those.

## How do I choose today's step?

Open your list and look at your energy, not your ambition. Then pick one of three sizes:

- **15 minutes** for a draining day: a quick edit, a short list, one photo.
- **30 minutes** for a normal day.
- **60 minutes** for a day when you have room.

Choose the step before the evening gets going. Many people do this the night before, or on their commute. When you sit down, you press start instead of deciding.

Set a timer. When it rings, stop, even if you want to keep going. Stopping on time makes tomorrow easier to begin.

On your worst days, shrink the step instead of skipping it. "Open the file and write one sentence" still counts, and it keeps the project warm. Starting is the hard part, and it often turns into more than you planned.

## How do I keep track of what I did?

Write one line after each step. A notebook page works. So does a note on your phone.

```
Mon: wrote title and description for mug one (25 min)
Tue: photographed mugs one and two (40 min)
Wed: skipped, late meeting
Thu: edited photos for mug one (15 min)
```

Two things happen as the list grows. You can see progress, which is the thing a long project usually hides. And you have a record to read when you feel like you are going nowhere. A missed day sits in the list without comment. It is information, not a failure.

This is why we prefer momentum to streaks. A streak resets to zero when life gets in the way. A log just keeps growing. If you want more on that, [working on a side project after work](/blog/side-project-after-work/) has ideas for fitting small steps around a full day.

## What should I look at in the weekly review?

Once a week, take ten minutes to answer three questions:

1. What did I finish this week?
2. What got in the way, or what did I keep putting off?
3. What are the next three steps?

Cross-check the steps against your finish line. If a step does not lead toward it, move it to the "later" list. This is where scope creep gets caught: not in the heat of the moment, but in a calm look back.

The weekly review is also where you update your estimate. If the list shows 24 steps left and you manage four a week, you are about six weeks out. That is a real date you can plan around, and it is usually closer than it feels.

## What if I miss days or lose interest?

You will. Everyone does, and the method is designed for it.

- **Missed one day:** do the next step. Do not double up.
- **Missed a week:** make your next step the smallest one on the list, such as reopening the file and reading your last log line.
- **Lost interest:** check whether the steps got vague. Interest usually returns when the next step is clear and small. If it does not, it is fine to decide the project is done or paused. Write that down too.

Rest is part of the plan. Taking one evening off a week makes the other evenings easier.
