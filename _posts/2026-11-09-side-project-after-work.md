Every guide to side projects seems to assume you have a free Saturday and boundless energy. Most people don’t. Most people have a job, a commute, dinner to make, maybe kids to put to bed, and then a small, tired window at the end of the day.

The good news: that window is enough. Plenty of apps, shops, newsletters and channels were built entirely in it. The trick is to stop treating it like a smaller version of a workday and design for what it actually is: short, low-energy and easily interrupted.

- Plan sessions for your tired self, not your best self
- Keep sessions short: 15, 30 or 60 minutes
- Decide the step the day before, so you never start by deciding
- Protect one fixed slot instead of “whenever I get time”
- Judge progress by the week, not the day
{: .summary}

## Plan for your tired self, not your best self

When you plan your project on a Sunday afternoon, you’re rested and optimistic, and you write tasks for that person: “redesign the landing page”, “record two videos”.

But the person who has to do those tasks is you on Wednesday at 9:15 pm, after a long day. That person needs something different: a task that’s clear, small and doesn’t require big decisions.

A useful habit: sort your project tasks by energy, not just by importance.

- **Low-energy tasks** (for tired evenings): replying to comments, editing yesterday’s draft, uploading product photos, fixing a small bug you already understand, writing three headline options.
- **High-energy tasks** (for your best hours): deciding on pricing, writing something from scratch, designing the core feature, recording.

Keep a short list of low-energy tasks ready. On a bad day, you pick one and you still move forward.

## Keep sessions short on purpose

Long sessions sound productive, but they have two big problems after work. They’re hard to start (“I don’t have two hours tonight”, so you do nothing), and they eat into sleep, which makes tomorrow’s session worse.

Short sessions flip this. A 15-minute task is almost always possible, even on a bad night. A 30-minute task fits after dinner. An hour is a good night.

Pick the size before you start: 15, 30 or 60 minutes. Then stop when the time’s up, even if you’re on a roll. Stopping mid-flow sounds wrong, but it makes the next session easier to start, because you know exactly where you left off.
{: .tip}

## Decide the step the day before

The hardest part of an evening session is often the first five minutes, spent deciding what to work on. That decision takes energy you don’t have, and it’s where a lot of evenings quietly turn into scrolling.

So move the decision earlier. At the end of each session, or in the morning on your commute, pick the next step and write it down somewhere you’ll see it:

> Tomorrow: write the product description for the blue mug. 30 minutes.

Now the evening session starts with doing, not deciding. This is the single most useful change most after-work builders can make.

## Protect one slot instead of “whenever”

“Whenever I have time” means never, because there’s always something else. A fixed slot works better, even a small one:

- **Early bird:** 30 minutes before everyone else wakes up. Works well if your evenings are unpredictable.
- **Lunch break:** 20 minutes twice a week for low-energy tasks like replying to customers.
- **After the kids are down:** 8:30 to 9:15, three nights a week.
- **One longer weekend block:** 90 minutes on Saturday morning for the high-energy work.

Pick one and try it for two weeks before you judge it. Tell the people you live with, so it becomes part of the household routine instead of something you have to defend each night.

## Make the work easy to start

Small bits of friction add up fast when you’re tired. Remove them:

- Leave the project open where you can resume it in one click.
- Keep a “start here” note at the top of your project doc with tomorrow’s step.
- Put your phone in another room for the session, or at least face down.
- Set a timer. A visible countdown makes it easier to begin and easier to stop.

## Judge progress by the week, not the day

Some evenings you’ll be too wiped to do anything. That’s fine, and it’s not failure. If you judge every single day, a bad day feels like you’ve broken something, and that guilt makes the next day harder too.

Look at the week instead. Three or four short sessions a week, every week, is a strong pace. Over three months that’s around 40 sessions, which is enough to launch a store, publish a dozen videos or ship a first version of an app.

At the end of each week, spend five minutes looking back: what did you finish, what got in the way, and what are the three most useful steps for next week? That short review is where you learn what actually works for your life.

## When “tired” is really something else

Sometimes tiredness is a sign the plan is wrong, not that you need to push harder. If you’ve been dreading the project for weeks, ask:

- **Is the next step too big?** Shrink it until it’s obvious.
- **Is the project still the right one?** It’s fine to park it and pick something you care about more.
- **Are you running on empty?** Rest is part of the plan. A project you return to in a week beats one you resent.

## A simple routine to start tonight

1. Write one sentence about what “done” looks like for your project’s first version.
2. Pick tomorrow’s step and its size: 15, 30 or 60 minutes.
3. Choose your slot and put it in your calendar.
4. Do the step, stop when the timer ends, and write down the next one.
5. On Sunday, spend five minutes looking back at the week.
