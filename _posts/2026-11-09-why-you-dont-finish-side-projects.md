If you have a folder of half-built apps, a store with three products and no launch, or a channel with four videos and a long pause, you’re in very good company. Ask any group of people who build things after work and most will admit to a graveyard of side projects.

The usual explanation is motivation: you just didn’t want it badly enough. That explanation is wrong, and it’s also unhelpful, because “want it more” isn’t something you can do on a Tuesday night.

When you look at why side projects actually stall, the same four reasons come up again and again. None of them are about character. All of them are fixable.

- You never decided what “done” looks like
- The next step is too big or too vague to start
- Nobody notices when you stop
- One missed week turns into a missed month
{: .summary}

## 1. You never decided what “done” looks like

At work, projects have a finish line someone else drew: a launch date, a client handoff, a deadline. Side projects don’t. You start with an exciting idea, and the idea keeps growing as you work on it.

That’s how a “simple habit app” quietly turns into an app with accounts, sync, social features and a web dashboard. Each addition is reasonable. Together they push the finish line so far out that you can’t see it any more, and a project with no visible end is very easy to put down.

**The fix:** write one sentence that describes the first finished version, in terms you could show another person.

- Not “launch my store”, but “five products listed, with photos, and one paid order.”
- Not “grow my channel”, but “ten videos published, one a week.”
- Not “build the app”, but “a friend can sign up and log their first workout without my help.”

Then go through your plans and ask of each item: is this needed for that sentence to be true? If not, it goes on a “later” list. You’re not deleting the ideas. You’re just not letting them block the finish line.

## 2. The next step is too big or too vague to start

Look at your to-do list for your project. If it says things like “work on the website”, “marketing” or “fix the app”, that’s the problem.

A vague task forces you to decide what to do every time you sit down, and deciding is tiring. After a full day of work, “work on the website” loses to the sofa almost every time. Not because you’re lazy, but because your brain sensibly avoids a job with no clear beginning.

**The fix:** shrink the next step until it’s specific and small enough to start in two minutes and finish in one sitting.

| Vague | Specific and small |
| --- | --- |
| Work on the website | Write the three-line headline for the home page |
| Marketing | Reply to five comments under my last post |
| Fix the app | Reproduce the sign-in bug and write down the steps |
| Start the newsletter | Pick a name and write the first subject line |

There’s good research behind this. Psychologists Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran reviewed dozens of studies on “implementation intentions”, plans in the form *“when X happens, I will do Y”*, and found they consistently help people follow through on goals they already hold. A specific step at a specific time beats a big intention.

A small, specific step done counts for more than a big, vague one planned. Pick the step tonight so tomorrow-you doesn’t have to decide.
{: .tip}

## 3. Nobody notices when you stop

This is the one people underestimate. At work, if you quietly stop working on something, someone notices within a day or two. On a side project, you can stop for six weeks and the only person who knows is you.

That’s not a willpower problem. Humans are social. We follow through more reliably when someone else is expecting something from us, even if the “someone” is a friend who asks “how did it go?” once a week.

**The fix:** add one small source of outside accountability. It doesn’t have to be dramatic or public.

- A friend you text every Sunday with one line: what you did, and what’s next.
- A small group of other builders with a weekly check-in.
- A coaching app that asks how it went each evening and remembers the answer.

## 4. One missed week turns into a missed month

Life happens. A busy week at work, a sick kid, a trip. You miss a few days.

Then something sneaky happens: coming back feels harder than starting did. You’ve forgotten where you left off. You feel a bit guilty. If you were keeping a streak, it’s broken, and a broken streak makes it feel like there’s nothing left to protect. A week becomes a month.

**The fix:** make coming back easy and judgment-free.

- **Leave yourself a note** at the end of each session: “Next: write the pricing section.” Future-you will thank you.
- **Make the comeback step tiny.** After a break, don’t try to catch up. Do one 15-minute thing to get back in motion.
- **Track momentum, not streaks.** A streak resets to zero and punishes you for being human. A running record of everything you’ve done (a simple log in a notebook works) shows the progress that’s still there.

## What actually gets side projects finished

Put the four fixes together and you get a simple system that works on tired evenings, not just inspired weekends:

1. **One sentence** that defines your first finished version.
2. **One small, specific step a day**, chosen the day before.
3. **One check-in** with someone (or something) that notices.
4. **A record of progress** that forgives missed days.

None of this needs more motivation. It needs less friction. Motivation tends to show up *after* you start, not before, so the whole game is making starting easy.
