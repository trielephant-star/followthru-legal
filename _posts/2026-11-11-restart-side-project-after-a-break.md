You meant to work on it last week. Then work got busy, someone got sick, or you simply ran out of evenings. Now it's been three weeks, or three months, and the project sits there like a gym bag you keep stepping around.

Here's the good news: this is one of the most normal things that happens to side projects. Nothing is broken. The way back is smaller than you think, and you don't need a big comeback day to do it.

- Skip the catch-up. You're not behind, you're just resuming
- Spend your first session on a 15-minute re-entry step, not real work
- Write a note for future you before you stop, every time
- Come back at half size for a week, then grow the plan again
{: .summary}

## Why coming back feels harder than starting

Two things make restarting heavy, and neither is a character flaw.

**You lost the thread.** When you stepped away, the details of what you were doing, and why, faded. Now sitting down means rebuilding all that context before you can make any progress. That's real mental work, so avoiding it is a very reasonable instinct.

**It feels a bit awkward.** Maybe there's a little guilt, or a feeling that you have to explain yourself to your own project. That feeling makes the project more important than it needs to be, and big things are harder to start.

Both problems have the same fix: make the first step so small and so clearly defined that there's nothing to dread.

## Your first session back: the 15-minute re-entry

Forget real progress for now. Your only job today is to get your head back inside the project. Set a timer for 15 minutes and follow this routine:

1. **Look around (5 minutes).** Open the project. Read your last notes, scroll your recent work, check the to-do list. Don't fix anything.
2. **Write the state of things (5 minutes).** Three lines: what exists, what's broken or missing, and what the finish line was. Plain words are fine.
3. **Pick one small next step (5 minutes).** Something you could finish in 30 minutes or less, specific enough to start without thinking. Write it down. Then stop, or keep going if you feel like it.

That's it. If you did those three things, you've restarted. Anything after is a bonus.

| Project type | Re-entry look-around | A good first small step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Online shop | Open your listings and orders | Improve the photos on one listing |
| Video channel | Reread your script ideas | Write the first 30 seconds of the next video |
| App | Run it once and note what breaks | Fix one bug you can reproduce |
| Newsletter | Reread your last issue | Draft three subject lines |

## Check the finish line, then trim

A break is a natural moment to ask whether the plan still fits your life. You might have less time now, or your idea might have shifted.

Look at your finish line sentence (the one that says what "first finished version" means) and ask two questions:

- Is this still what I want to make?
- Can I get there with the time I realistically have?

If the answer to the second is no, shrink the plan, don't abandon it. Move extras to a "later" list and keep the part that proves the idea. If you never wrote a finish line, our guide on [why side projects don't get finished](/blog/why-you-dont-finish-side-projects/) shows how to write one in a sentence.

## Ease back in for a week

After a long break, resist the urge to make up for lost time. A heroic first weekend usually ends with exhaustion and another break.

Try a half-size week instead:

- Work in 15 to 30 minute sessions rather than long ones.
- Aim for three or four sessions, not seven.
- Pick the next step the evening before, so you don't have to decide when you're tired.
- Leave a note at the end of each session.

If you're working around a job, [working on your side project after work](/blog/side-project-after-work/) has more ideas for tired evenings. After a week, you can raise the size of the steps again if it feels good.

A note for future you is the best gift you can give. At the end of each session, write one line: "Did X. Next: Y." Tomorrow, or in three weeks, you won't have to rebuild anything. You'll just read the line and start.
{: .tip}

## What to do about the guilt

If you feel bad about the gap, notice it, then let it go. The project doesn't keep score. It's not angry with you. Nobody is waiting for an apology, including you.

What helps instead is evidence that progress still exists. Keep a short running list of things you've finished: pages built, listings published, videos recorded, bugs fixed. When you've been away, that list reminds you the work you did is still there. A streak resets to zero when you miss days. A log of finished things doesn't.

And rest is part of the plan. Some breaks happen because you needed one.

## How to make the next break less painful

You can't prevent busy weeks, but you can make them cheaper:

- **Keep a "next step" note** updated, always.
- **Have a minimum version of a work week**: one 15-minute task you can do on your worst day.
- **Tell one person** you're building something, so someone might ask how it's going. We cover options in [finding an accountability partner](/blog/accountability-partner-for-side-projects/).
- **Notice early.** If you've skipped three days, do a 15-minute re-entry then, not after three weeks.
