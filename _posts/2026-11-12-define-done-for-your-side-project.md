Most side projects don’t have a finish line. They have a direction. “Build a mug shop.” “Make a cooking channel.” “Finish my app.” You can walk in that direction for a year and never arrive, because there’s no place to stop.

That’s why a project can feel eighty percent done for months. Every time you get close, a new idea moves the goalposts. The fix isn’t more willpower. It’s deciding, once, what “done” means, and writing it down while you’re calm and not in the middle of the work.

- Write your finish line as one sentence a stranger could check
- Make it something you can show: a link, a video, a listing, a build
- Test each feature with: can the first user get the result without it?
- Put every new idea on a later list instead of into the project
- Give the first version a rough size: four to eight weeks of short sessions
{: .summary}

## Write the finish line in one sentence

Your finish line should be something you could point at. “The app is polished” can’t be checked. “A friend can install the app and log a workout on their own phone” can.

Try this template: **“Done means [a specific person] can [do one specific thing] by [a rough date].”**

Some examples for the kinds of builders we hear from most:

| Project | Vague goal | Finish line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mug shop | “Launch my shop” | Five mugs listed with photos and descriptions, and the shop open to the public |
| Cooking channel | “Grow a channel” | Three videos published with a consistent title format |
| Workout app | “Build my fitness app” | A friend can sign up and log one workout on their phone |
| Newsletter | “Start a newsletter” | Issue one sent to 20 people I know |

If you can’t write it in one sentence, the project is probably two projects. Pick the one you’d be proud to show first.

## Decide what the first version is not

A finish line says what’s in. Just as useful is a short list of what’s out. Write three things you are deliberately not doing yet:

- Not building a mobile version until the web version works
- Not designing a logo until the first five sales
- Not editing videos with fancy transitions

This sounds small, but it’s the thing you’ll read the next time you feel the urge to add something. It turns “should I add this?” into “is this on the out list?”, which is a much easier question to answer when you’re tired.

## Cut features with two questions

When your list is too long, go through it one item at a time and ask:

1. **Could the first user still get the main result without this?** If yes, it’s not in version one.
2. **Could I do this by hand for now?** An email you send yourself beats an automated email system you spend two weeks building. A spreadsheet beats a dashboard.

Be kind but firm. You’re not deleting ideas, you’re scheduling them for later.

## Keep a later list

The reason scope creeps is that a good idea feels urgent. If you don’t write it somewhere, you’ll feel like you have to build it now or lose it.

So make a note called “Later” and add every idea there in one line. That’s it. No sorting, no planning. The act of writing it down tells your brain it’s safe, and you can keep working on the real finish line.

Once you ship, open the list and pick the best one or two. You’ll often find half of them no longer seem important. That’s useful information too.

End each session by reading your finish line aloud, or at least once in your head. It takes five seconds, and it makes “what should I work on?” much easier to answer.
{: .tip}

## Turn the finish line into small steps

A finish line is a destination, not a to-do list. Work backwards from it until the steps fit in one sitting:

- Finish line: five mugs listed and the shop open.
- Needs: five photos, five descriptions, five prices, a shop name, a policy page.
- Tonight’s step (30 minutes): write the description for mug one.

The best step is one where you already know the first action. If you’d have to think about where to begin, shrink it again. If you’re short on energy in the evenings, the [after-work guide](/blog/side-project-after-work/) has ideas for matching steps to the time and energy you really have.

## When the finish line moves anyway

Sometimes you learn something real halfway through. A friend tries your prototype and finds the main idea confusing. Maybe the shop needs a shipping policy after all. That’s allowed.

The rule is simple: change the finish line on purpose, not by accident. Stop, rewrite the sentence, and ask what you’re dropping to make room. If you add something, something else leaves, or the date moves. Then carry on.

If you keep losing steam anyway, that’s usually not a defining-done problem. [Why you don’t finish side projects](/blog/why-you-dont-finish-side-projects/) covers the other common reasons.

## A routine you can do tonight

1. Write your finish line using the template. One sentence.
2. Write three “not yet” items.
3. Open a note called Later and move every extra idea there.
4. List what version one needs, then cut with the two questions.
5. Pick tomorrow’s step and give it a size: 15, 30 or 60 minutes.
